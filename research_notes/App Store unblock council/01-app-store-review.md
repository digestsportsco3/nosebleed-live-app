## Key Question 1 — Guidelines 5.2.1 / 5.2.2 (intellectual property): what Apple accepts as "permission," whether a no-logo redesign satisfies review, and what the reply/appeal should say

### Takeaway
Apple's rule is written as a strict-liability rule ("Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks ... without permission") plus a documentation hook ("Authorization must be provided upon request"); the only forms of "permission" that reviewers are documented accepting are a written license/authorization from the rights holder or, per experienced forum posters, a counsel's letter in Review Notes — a disclaimer is explicitly not enough. There is no Apple-published safe harbor for team names/abbreviations/colors, but the guideline targets "protected third-party material," and every forum case found involved logos/league marks; a logo-free build (text names, abbreviations, team colors, your own iconography) is the standard remediation advised on Apple's forums, and no documented case of Apple rejecting a sports-data app for names-and-colors alone was found.

### Cited Findings
- Verbatim 5.2.1 (page "Last Updated: October 16, 2026"): "Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas in your app without permission, and don't include misleading, false, or copycat representations, names, or metadata in your app bundle or developer name. Apps should be submitted by the person or legal entity that owns or has licensed the intellectual property and other relevant rights." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Verbatim 5.2.2: "If your app uses, accesses, monetizes access to, or displays content from a third-party service, ensure that you are specifically permitted to do so under the service's terms of use. Authorization must be provided upon request." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Nov 13, 2025 guideline update added 4.1(c): no use of another developer's icon, brand, or name without approval (a separate, developer-vs-developer rule; shows Apple's trend toward explicit brand-permission rules). — [Apple Developer News, Nov 13 2025 "Updated App Review Guidelines"](https://developer.apple.com/news/)
- Apple Developer Forums thread (2018, "Rejected: advise on copyright issues"): a soccer fixtures/scores app was rejected repeatedly for "copyrighted data"; developer removed team and league logos and rejections *continued* because the reviewer also objected to the schedule/results data; reviewer never answered Resolution Center questions. ### Inferences
- "Permission" for 5.2.1/5.2.2 purposes means a document you can upload in Resolution Center: a league/team license, a data-provider license whose terms expressly cover logo display in apps, or a signed counsel letter. Nosebleed has none, and ESPN's CDN is not a licensed source; ESPN's terms (unfetched) almost certainly do not grant third-party commercial app use of its hosted logo files. So the only defensible "permission" path is to license logos (e.g., from a sports-data vendor that sublicenses official marks) — slow and expensive — or to remove them.
- The redesign path is the fast one: ship with *zero* league marks, team logos, and player headshots; render team identity as text (city/nickname), abbreviation and the existing colored-circle fallback; use original Nosebleed iconography for sport tabs. The colored-circle-with-abbreviation fallback already exists in the UI, so the change is a config flip plus removing `LEAGUE_LOGOS` and the headshot provider.
- Because Apple's rejection named the NFL only, do not assume other leagues are cleared; the Nov 2025 forum pattern (reviewers citing data-provider ToS) means the reviewer may next ask about the ESPN data feed itself. Review Notes should pre-empt this by describing the data as factual scores/odds, with no league-produced content, and naming the data sources.
- Team names/abbreviations used descriptively to report scores are classic nominative use; no case was found of Apple rejecting a scores app for text names (the 2018 thread's continued rejection was about FIFA *schedule data*, not names). Keep the app name and metadata free of league names ("NFL picks") to avoid 5.2.1's "copycat ... names, or metadata" clause and the 2.3.x metadata rules.
- Suggested Resolution Center reply (after shipping the logo-free build): "We have removed all league marks, team logos and player images from the binary (build X). Teams are now identified only by name, abbreviation and a generic color chip generated by the app; sport sections use original Nosebleed artwork. The app displays factual scores, schedules and odds obtained from [sources]; it contains no league-produced video, audio or editorial content and makes no claim of affiliation with any league. Screenshots of the new team-chip rendering are attached. We believe build X complies with Guideline 5.2.1 and 5.2.2; please let us know if any specific asset remains of concern." Do not file an App Review Board appeal on the original (logo) build — the forum guidance is to fix and resubmit, and appeals are for cases where the reviewer misapplied the rule.

### Gaps
- No Apple-authored statement specifically about sports logos exists; the only public evidence is forum threads with unrecorded outcomes. I found no confirmed, documented case of a sports app approved after logo removal (the closest, thread 718427, resubmitted without the FIFA mark and never reported back).
- ESPN's current Terms of Use and any ESPN developer-API terms were not retrievable; whether ESPN's undocumented `site.api.espn.com` endpoint is permitted for commercial app use (relevant to 5.2.2) is unverified.
- Nominative-fair-use case law search was not run (search budget exhausted); the legal (as opposed to App Review) defensibility of names/abbreviations/colors should be confirmed by counsel.

## Key Question 2 — Guidelines 3.1.1 / 3.1.3 and the Epic injunction: can a US app link to a web checkout for the "Betting Pass" with no IAP and no commission as of October 2026? What about outside the US?

### Takeaway
Yes for the US storefront, today: since Apple's May 1, 2025 guideline change (compelled by the April 30, 2025 contempt order), US-storefront apps may include "buttons, external links, or other calls to action" to outside purchase methods with no entitlement, and Apple is currently charging 0% on those link-outs; the Ninth Circuit (Dec 11, 2025) affirmed the contempt finding but held the district court went too far in barring *any* commission, so a court-set commission (Apple has proposed up to 15%) is pending on remand, and the Supreme Court (cert granted June 30, 2026, limited to the contempt standard; briefing through Nov 13, 2026; no argument date) could unsettle the regime in 2027. Outside the US the default rule still bars link-outs to non-IAP purchasing except where a regional entitlement/regime applies (EU DMA, Japan MSCA, Brazil), each with its own fee.

### Inferences
- The second rejection point (external purchase path) is explainable in two ways and the team should find out which before resubmitting: (1) the binary was reviewed as available in non-US storefronts, where 3.1.1 still bars the link; or (2) the reviewer treated the link as a 3.1.1 violation out of habit/misreading. In either case the fix is the same: restrict availability to the US storefront in App Store Connect (or gate the card by `Storefront.current`/App Store account storefront in the wrapper and hide it elsewhere), and cite 3.1.1 and 3.1.1(a)'s US-storefront language in Review Notes.
- Under the Oct 16, 2026 text, a US-only app may show the Whop link with no entitlement, no Apple disclosure sheet and (today) no commission. The link must not be "misleading" (3.1.1(a) fraud clause) and the app must still be more than a storefront for the website (4.2, see Q4). Nothing in the US carve-out exempts the app from 2.3.1 (describe the purchase flow in Review Notes) or from 5.3 (Q4).
- Commission risk is forward-looking, not current: the remand will yield a court-set rate (Apple asked for ≤15%; Epic says ~0%), and the Supreme Court decision (2027 at the earliest) could vacate the contempt finding and revive Apple's earlier 27%/12% link-out program. Any 2026 business plan should model a 0–15% link-out fee and the possibility of a return to a "StoreKit External Purchase Link Entitlement" regime (link-once, disclosure sheet, 7-day attribution, reporting) if Apple prevails. Treat 15% as an estimate of the ceiling Apple itself proposed, not a ruling.
- Non-US: if the team ever expands storefronts, the link must be removed or replaced per region (EU: alternative-payment terms with 5% CTC from Oct 1, 2026; Japan/Brazil: Attachment 12 terms; UK: pending CMA steering requirement; everywhere else: IAP only). The simplest compliant posture is US-only availability.
- Review Notes draft for the purchase path: "This app is distributed on the United States storefront only (availability restricted in App Store Connect). Per Guidelines 3.1.1 and 3.1.1(a), US-storefront apps may include buttons and external links to purchasing mechanisms other than in-app purchase without an entitlement. ## Key Question 3 — If the team chooses IAP: commission rates, Small Business Program eligibility, and coexistence with the Whop web subscriber base

### Takeaway
IAP costs 30% standard / 15% under the Small Business Program (≤$1M prior-year and current-year proceeds across all associated accounts) and 15% on auto-renewing subscriptions after one year; the clean coexistence pattern Apple's text allows is "sell the Betting Pass as an IAP *and* let web purchasers sign in" (3.1.3(b) Multiplatform Services), which on the US storefront can additionally carry the web link.

### Cited Findings
- Small Business Program: 15% commission on paid apps and IAP; eligibility requires you and all Associated Developer Accounts earned no more than $1M USD in proceeds (net of commission, certain taxes and adjustments) in the previous calendar year and in the current year; exceeding $1M in-year moves future sales to the standard rate; re-qualify the year after falling back below; enrollment requires the Account Holder to accept the latest Paid Apps agreement (Schedule 2) and list Associated Developer Accounts; adjusted proceeds begin 15 days after the end of the fiscal month in which enrollment is approved. — [Apple App Store Small Business Program](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/)

## Key Question 4 — Guideline 5.3 (gambling), offshore sportsbook links, picks/tipster apps, and other hidden rejection risks in this app

### Takeaway
5.3.4 regulates apps that "offer real money gaming" (license, geo-restriction, free) and does not expressly address advertising or linking to sportsbooks, but Apple's stock rejection text ("facilitates, enables, or encourages gambling activity ... not legal in all of the locations where your app is available") and documented 5.3.4 rejections for gambling *banner ads* show reviewers apply it to promotional links; five outbound links to offshore books (bovada.lv, mybookie.ag, betonline.ag, betus.com.pa, lowvig.ag) plus Polymarket/Kalshi market links are therefore a live rejection and post-approval removal risk on the US storefront, and must be replaced with licensed-operator links (or no links) in the resubmission. Separately, the PWA wrapper (4.2), the undocumented ESPN API (5.2.2), player headshots (5.2.1), the "verified picks" metadata claim (2.3.1), the 18+ age rating and the missing in-app privacy policy link are all visible, fixable risks.

### Cited Findings
- Verbatim 5.3 preamble: "Gaming, gambling, and lotteries can be tricky to manage and tend to be one of the most regulated offerings on the App Store. Only include this functionality if you've fully vetted your legal obligations everywhere you make your app available and are prepared for extra time during the review process." 5.3.3: "Apps may not use in-app purchase to purchase credit or currency for use in conjunction with real money gaming of any kind." 5.3.4: "Apps that offer real money gaming (such as sports betting, poker, casino games, horse racing) or lotteries must have necessary licensing and permissions in the locations where the app is used, must be geo-restricted to those locations, and must be free on the App Store. Illegal gambling aids, including card counters, are not permitted on the App Store." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple's stock 5.3 rejection text (quoted by developers in 2017 and again in 2023): "Your app contains gambling content or facilitates, enables, or encourages gambling activity. In the 2023 case Apple demanded (1) documentary proof of licensing for every selected country/region and (2) geo-restriction via App Store Connect availability, warning the app "will be removed from any store where the developer lacks the required license." ### Inferences
- Offshore links are the biggest *removal* risk even if the app slips through review: 5.3's preamble says the developer must have "fully vetted your legal obligations everywhere," Apple's template language targets apps that "encourage gambling activity ... not legal in all of the locations," and Bovada/BetOnline/MyBookie/BetUS/LowVig hold no US state licenses (common knowledge in the industry; the planned state-regulator cease-and-desist search did not run — see Gaps). An App Review reviewer tapping a BetUS link and landing on an offshore sportsbook, in an app whose own footer says "21+ ... 1-800-GAMBLER," is a plausible 5.3.4 rejection and a plausible 2.3.1/5.3 removal later. The defensible fix is to show odds only from US-licensed operators (e.g., books with state licenses) or to strip the outbound `book-link` hrefs entirely and present books as unlinked labels; drop the BetUS "sponsor" treatment from the iOS build.
- Polymarket/Kalshi links: Kalshi is a CFTC-regulated exchange and Polymarket's US status has been in flux; both are "event contracts" that a reviewer may read as "real money gaming (such as sports betting)." Remove or hide these links in the iOS build for the resubmission; reintroduce only with counsel sign-off and a Review Notes explanation. (Regulatory status not researched here — see Gaps.)
- The app is a picks/odds *information* product, which the market shows is approvable (Action Network, OddsJam, BettingPros, tipster apps) when it (a) takes no wagers, (b) says so, (c) carries an 18+ rating, and (d) links, if at all, to licensed operators. Nosebleed should declare "Gambling" = none/"contests" as appropriate but expect the 18+ rating given betting content; set the age rating honestly rather than 4+/13+ (misrating is itself a 2.3 issue).
- 4.2 web-wrapper risk is real but secondary to the three explicit issues; mitigate by adding at least one native capability in the wrapper (push notifications for picks/line moves, offline cached scores from the existing service worker, haptics/native share) and by describing those in Review Notes. If the wrapper is Capacitor, do not rely on a remote URL load; bundle the web assets.
- 5.2.2 exposure from ESPN: the data proxy (`site.api.espn.com`) is an undocumented endpoint; even after removing logos, a reviewer who asks "do you have permission to use ESPN data?" must get an answer. The defensible path is a licensed data feed (the odds already come from a licensed API; scores/schedules should follow) or, at minimum, a Review Notes statement naming the sources and the facts-only nature of the data. Player headshots are both a 5.2.1 (photo copyright) and a publicity-rights issue; remove them from the iOS build.
- Metadata: change "verified picks" in `manifest.json`/App Store copy unless the team can substantiate "verified" (third-party-tracked record); keep "expert picks." Include a privacy-policy URL in App Store Connect and a visible link in the app (5.1.1(i) requires it; not quoted here because that section was not extracted — treat as standard).
