## Key Question 1: Why the offshore association kills league data/marks deals (league policies, official-data-partner requirements, AGA position, 2024–2026 enforcement)

### Takeaway
Every US major-league betting-rights structure (NFL/NBA/MLB/NHL/NCAA) funnels data and marks only to operators that are state-licensed, agree to integrity terms, and buy official data from the league's exclusive provider (Genius for NFL, Sportradar for NHL/NBA/MLB); the data providers publicly state they verify licenses against regulators and restrict gambling advertising in their media-data terms. Meanwhile BetUS, Bovada, MyBookie and BetOnline are the named targets of a 2024–2026 wave of state cease-and-desist orders that characterize their US operations as felonies, so any media/app property that links to them is, from a rights-holder's point of view, distributing traffic to an illegal operator. No source confirms the specific reason Nosebleed was denied; the inference that the BetUS link was the trigger is strongly consistent with the evidence but remains an inference.

## Key Question 2: Legal exposure to Nosebleed itself for promoting BetUS to US users (state advertising/affiliate rules, Wire Act/UIGEA aiding theories, FTC, misdemeanor/felony risk)

### Takeaway
The direct criminal statutes (Wire Act, UIGEA, state "offering internet gaming" felonies) are written against the operator, so Nosebleed's exposure runs through accomplice/"promoting gambling" theories: federal aiding-and-abetting (DOJ has used this threat against advertisers before), and state "promoting gambling" offenses whose "advancing" element explicitly includes soliciting or inducing participation (NY: class A misdemeanor; NJ: disorderly persons offense up to $10,000, graded higher for bookmaking). I found no reported US prosecution of a media company or affiliate for linking to an offshore sportsbook; the realistic exposure is regulatory (loss of affiliate licensability, operator program termination, AG consumer-protection letters) and reputational, with a non-zero misdemeanor tail risk in New York and New Jersey in particular.

### Cited Findings

Federal theories
- Aiding and abetting: "An accomplice who aids and abets another in the commission of a federal crime may be treated as if he had committed the crime himself" (Nye & Nissen test: joins the venture and seeks to make it succeed); DOJ "advised the National Association of Broadcasters that its members risked prosecution for aiding and abetting when they provided advertising for the online gambling operations." — [CRS RS21984 (2012)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20120124_RS21984_f13cb1f61558b2318f016fd06123119d14fe617c.html); [CRS RS21984 (2004)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20041129_RS21984_620c2031158ac7fc3326004a6710a9fb21aa6ddf.html)
- Standard as described by a gaming-law firm: liability "if the party who produces, accepts and runs the ads knows the illegal nature of the site being advertised and provides substantial assistance or encouragement"; a quick look at the site can show it is unlicensed, undercutting a "didn't know" defense. — [Walters Law Group](https://www.firstamendment.com/online-casino-risk/)
- Wire Act scope: DOJ OLC concluded "interstate transmissions of wire communications that do not relate to a 'sporting event or contest,' fall outside the reach of the Wire Act"; "the vast majority of prosecutions have involved sports gambling." — [CRS RS21984](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20120124_RS21984_f13cb1f61558b2318f016fd06123119d14fe617c.html); statute text at [18 U.S.C. 1084](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1084) (not fetchable from sandbox)
- UIGEA, 31 U.S.C. 5363: prohibits a person "engaged in the business of betting or wagering" from knowingly accepting credit, EFTs, checks or similar instruments in connection with unlawful internet gambling. — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5363); [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/31/5363)
- Marketing-only firms have argued that services short of taking bets lack the overt act for conspiracy, but "the legal soundness of such claims has yet to be significantly tested in the courts." — [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppardmullin.com/media/publication/56_pub608.pdf)

State criminal "promoting gambling" statutes (the ones that can reach a promoter)
- New York Penal Law 225.05: "A person is guilty of promoting gambling in the second degree when he knowingly advances or profits from unlawful gambling activity"; class A misdemeanor. — [NY Senate](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.05); 225.10 first degree (class E felony) requires bookmaking thresholds. — [NY Public.Law](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.05)
- NYC Bar: the "advancing" standard is "a very broad one," covering conduct that "materially aids" illegal gambling including "the solicitation or inducement of persons to participate therein"; a 1999 NY Supreme Court decision held that advancing gambling over the internet, "even by a gambling operator legally licensed in another jurisdiction," is covered. — [NYC Bar PDF](https://www.nycbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/GamblingBill.pdf)
- NY Attorney General industry alert (Jan 30, 2026, re prediction markets): "the unlicensed conduct, advertisement, and promotion of sports wagering in New York ... violates the Racing Laws," and violators who "knowingly advance" gambling "may face criminal charges for promoting gambling in the first or second degree." — [NY AG PDF](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/prediction-market-industry-alert-oag-1.30.26-final.pdf)
- New Jersey N.J.S.A. 2C:37-2: promoting gambling includes "conduct that materially aids any form of gambling activity," with examples including "soliciting or inducing persons to participate"; baseline is a disorderly persons offense (fine up to $10,000); third degree (fine up to $35,000) for qualifying bookmaking; sources conflict on the bookmaking thresholds. — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2c/section-2c-37-2); [NJ Courts model charge](https://www.njcourts.gov/charges/promoting-gambling-conduct-materially-aids-gambling-activity); [vLex](https://us.vlex.com/vid/promoting-gambling-660659069)
- Michigan MCL 432.313 (Lawful Internet Gaming Act): offering internet gaming without being a licensed operator is a felony, up to 10 years and/or $100,000; MGCB press materials cite the parallel felony in the Gaming Control and Revenue Act. No current "advertising" prohibition for unlicensed operators found; 2025 SB 713 would require board approval of all internet-gaming ads (status unknown). — [MCL 432.313 PDF](https://legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/MCL-432-313.pdf); [MGCB illegal gaming page](https://www.michigan.gov/mgcb/about/illegal-gaming); [SB 713](https://hercules-milenia.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2025-2026/billintroduced/Senate/htm/2025-SIB-0713.htm)
- Florida: FGCC characterizes offshore sports wagering offered to Floridians as "a felony offense" under Florida law. — [FGCC PDF](https://flgaming.gov/docs/Press-Release/Press-Release-FGCC-Demands-Overseas-Bookmakers-and-Casinos-Cease-Illegal-Online-Gambling-Operations-Feb-2025.pdf)
- Massachusetts AG framed Bovada's "USA-based" marketing as a consumer-protection (Ch. 93A-type) violation, i.e., AGs use deceptive-marketing law, not only gambling law. — [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/news/attorney-generals-office-issues-cease-and-desist-letter-to-online-gambling-operator-for-illegal-offshore-casino-operation)

FTC endorsement rules
- The FTC obtained a consent order against two influencers who "deceptively endorsed an online gambling service" without disclosing they owned it; order requires clear and conspicuous disclosure of "any material connections"; a material connection is one that "might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement," judged from the "reasonable consumer" perspective; free product or payment counts. — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/ftc-state-ag/first-ftc-complaint-against-individual-influencers-settles/); [Ward and Smith](https://www.wardandsmith.com/article/the-ad-vantage-point-navigating-the-ftc-endorsement-guides-part-i); [Foster Garvey PDF](https://www.foster.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf-exports/disclosing-endorsements-on-social-media-general-considerations-for-ftc-compliance-by-brands-and-influencers-3263.pdf)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE on severity: For Nosebleed (a promoter, not a bet-taker), the most plausible charges are NY PL 225.05 (class A misdemeanor) and NJ 2C:37-2 at the disorderly-persons/fourth-degree level; the felony tiers in NY/NJ/MI/FL are written around bookmaking or operating, which Nosebleed does not do. Federal aiding-and-abetting of a Wire Act violation is theoretically a felony-level exposure (accomplice treated as principal) but DOJ has historically used it as a warning to advertisers rather than bringing cases against media; I found no 2024–2026 affiliate prosecutions.
- INFERENCE: The "knowing" element is hard for Nosebleed to contest: a paid sponsorship contract with a .pa-domain book that has been named in Michigan, Florida, Mississippi and Maryland regulator letters establishes knowledge far more clearly than an aggregator-inserted logo would.
- INFERENCE: Practically, the binding constraints are (1) a reviewer at a licensed operator or regulator treating Nosebleed as "unsuitable," which follows the NJ 2015 template (bar principals, report to other regulators), and (2) the FanDuel-style affiliate-program terms, which will not let Nosebleed carry both FanDuel/DraftKings and BetUS.
- INFERENCE: FTC exposure is independent of gambling law: if BetUS pays Nosebleed and Nosebleed's accounts present BetUS lines or links as editorial, the material connection must be disclosed on every post; this also applies to the Betting Pass (if staff bet their own picks or have operator relationships).

### Gaps
- No case found applying NY 225.05 / NJ 2C:37-2 to a pure advertiser or affiliate of an offshore sportsbook; the risk is doctrinal, not demonstrated by prosecutions.
- Could not fetch the enacted text of 18 U.S.C. 1084 or NY PL 225.00 definitions to quote verbatim.
- Current PA investigation fee amount, Michigan affiliate fee confirmation, and NY/Ohio affiliate licensing status as of 2026 were not found; the NY State Gaming Commission's affiliate rules were not located.
- State licensing timelines (weeks/months) were not found in any source; see estimates in Question 4.

## Key Question 3: Platform exposure (Apple 5.3, Google Play, Meta/X/TikTok/YouTube gambling policies for the 44 accounts)

### Takeaway
Apple's 5.3.4 requires "necessary licensing and permissions" for apps offering real-money gaming and 5.2.1 bars third-party trademarks without permission, but Apple's own 3.1.1(a) now expressly permits external purchase links on the US storefront, so the Whop link rejection deserves a direct appeal. Google Play is the sharper problem: its Real-Money Gambling policy lists "a dedicated sports odds tracker app containing integrated gambling ads linking to a sports betting site" as a violation and (newer text) bars "companion functionality" such as "sports score/odds/performance tracking" for real-money gambling. Every major social ad platform (Meta, TikTok, Google/YouTube) allows gambling ads only for licensed operators with prior certification, and Meta extends the permission requirement to affiliate/aggregator landing pages.

## Key Question 6: Does the "Betting Pass" (paid picks) need any license in the US, and what disclaimers are standard?

### Takeaway
Selling picks (a "tout service") is not licensed anywhere in the US that I could find: Nevada's 2019 SB 46 would have created tout-service registration but the Gaming Control Board's own office asked that the tout sections be stripped in committee and the current NRS 463 index shows no tout provision; no other state tout-licensing regime surfaced. Exposure instead comes from consumer-protection and FTC rules (misrepresented records, undisclosed material connections) and from Apple's 5.3.3/5.3.4 if the pass is characterized as real-money-gaming credit rather than editorial content. Standard disclaimers are: entertainment/informational only, no guarantee of profit, not a sportsbook/no wagers accepted, bettor solely responsible, affiliate disclosure, local-law and 21+ notice, responsible-gambling helpline.

### Cited Findings
- Nevada SB 46 (2019, prefiled Nov 19, 2018 for the Gaming Control Board) defined a "tout service" as a person who "for any form of compensation, fee or remuneration, provides advice or opinions relating to wagering on racing or sporting events" and would have had the Commission regulate registration and suitability. — [Nevada Legislature SB46 PDF](https://archive.leg.state.nv.us/Session/80th2019/Bills/SB/SB46.pdf); [Public Gaming](https://publicgaming.com/news-categories-m/sports-betting-daily-fantasy-sports/3919-nevada-senate-bill-46-would-require-the-nevada-gaming-commission-to-provide-by-regulation-for-the-operation-and-registration-of-tout-services-and-persons-associated-therewith)
- At the Feb 6, 2019 Senate Judiciary hearing the Board's office proposed stripping sections 1 and 2 (tout provisions), saying it did not believe they needed to be in statute at that time; Senate Amendment 12 shows the tout language bracketed (omitted). — [Senate Judiciary minutes PDF](https://archive.leg.state.nv.us/Session/80th2019/Minutes/Senate/JUD/Final/126.pdf); [Amendment 12 PDF](https://archive.leg.state.nv.us/Session/80th2019/Bills/Amendments/A_SB46_12.pdf)
- Before SB 46, touts "operated in Nevada unregulated by the Board and the Commission"; the current NRS Chapter 463 (rev. 2025) index shows no tout section. — [Mondaq "Nevada Touts in a Bout"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/gaming/761106/nevada-touts-in-a-bout); [NRS 463](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/nrs/nrs-463.html)
- Industry definition: a tout "sells picks often in exchange for an up-front payment," per-pick or package pricing; transparency about records is the recurring criticism. — [RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/betting/faq/what-are-sports-betting-touts-1d56d433)
- FTC consent order against influencers who deceptively endorsed an online gambling service without disclosing ownership; disclosure of material connections required. — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/ftc-state-ag/first-ftc-complaint-against-individual-influencers-settles/)
- Standard tout disclaimers in use: "strictly for informational and entertainment purposes"; "past performance ... does not guarantee future results"; "does not accept, place, or facilitate real money wagers"; "you are solely responsible for your betting decisions"; affiliate-link disclosure; "it is your duty to verify and comply with the current local laws"; helpline reference. — [OddsGuy disclaimer](https://oddsguy.ai/disclaimer); [Pickswise T&C](https://www.pickswise.com/terms-and-conditions/); [Sharp Football Analysis ToS](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/terms-of-service/); [Bet SHARP App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1523369762)
- Apple: 5.3.3 bars IAP for "credit or currency for use in conjunction with real money gaming"; 5.3.4 requires licensing and "must be free" for apps that "offer real money gaming"; 3.1.1(a) permits external purchase links on the US storefront. — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- NY AG (2026) treats unlicensed "advertisement, and promotion of sports wagering" as a Racing Law violation (context: prediction markets). — [NY AG PDF](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/prediction-market-industry-alert-oag-1.30.26-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- INFERENCE: The Betting Pass needs no gaming license; it is paid editorial content. It becomes a gaming-law problem only if it (1) steers buyers to an unlicensed book (the BetUS link next to the picks is the real hazard), (2) is sold as "credit" or bundled with wagering, or (3) makes record claims that are false (FTC Act §5 / state UDAP).
- INFERENCE for Apple: describe the pass in metadata and review notes as a subscription to analysis/editorial content, not as anything purchased "for use in conjunction with real money gaming"; keep no sportsbook deep-links on the pass screens; either use IAP or rely on 3.1.1(a) with a plain informational link on the US storefront only.
- INFERENCE: Standard operating disclosures for Nosebleed: 21+ (or 18+ where applicable) and state-eligibility notice; "for entertainment and informational purposes only; no guarantee of results"; "Nosebleed does not accept wagers"; verifiable, dated pick records; "#ad/#sponsored" and "we may earn a commission" on any operator link; 1-800-GAMBLER; and a statement that staff do not have financial relationships with any operator not licensed in a US state.

- No search surfaced any other state's tout-specific statute; absence of evidence here is reasonably strong but not exhaustive (search budget exhausted before a 50-state sweep).
- Whop's own seller terms/disclaimer requirements were not found.
