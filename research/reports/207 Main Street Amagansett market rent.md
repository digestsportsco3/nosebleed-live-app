## Executive Summary

**Answer: the current rent of $5,000/month ($60,000/year; $54.55/SF/year at 1,100 SF) is BELOW fair market rent, by a moderate rather than a dramatic margin.** The reconciled ESTIMATE of market rent is **$60–$72/SF/year NNN**, which implies **$5,500–$6,600/month** for 1,100 SF, with a best-supported midpoint of **$66/SF, or $6,050/month**. On that basis the existing rent is **about 17% below the market midpoint and about 9% below the low end of the range**. If the lease turns out to be gross (landlord pays taxes, insurance and building costs), the gap widens to about 25% on a gross-equivalent basis. These are calculations from the adjusted comparables in this report, not executed Amagansett rents, because no executed Amagansett commercial rent has been publicly disclosed for 2014–2026.

The **strongest defensible evidence** for the landlord is, in order: (1) this exact leasehold was marketed in December 2016 for **$625,000 in key money** for the "long-term lease" and equipment, later quoted at **$575,000**, and was assigned to Organic Krush on **June 12, 2017** after drawing "several serious inquiries" within a day [10][13][15], which shows that informed market participants valued occupancy of these premises above the contract rent then in force; (2) the only current Amagansett restaurant offering, 74A Montauk Highway, ASKS **$240,000/year NNN ($84.81/SF) plus $700,000 key money** [28][30]; (3) dry, non-food space in the Main Street district at 11 Indian Wells Highway ASKS **$65/SF NNN** [33]; (4) the local market is tight, with an Amagansett Square restaurant renewal offered at "almost triple" the prior rent in 2021 [76], few village vacancies since COVID [100], landlords "able to choose what tenants they want" [101] and Suffolk County sales tax up **9.2%** in the first half of 2026 [103]; and (5) restaurant and take-out food use are permitted as of right in the Central Business (CB) district under the 2018 use table [5], while the space was described as one of "a finite number of permitted 'wet uses'" in the Town [10].

The evidence is **thinner than an asking-rent reading would suggest**, and this report does not overstate it. The **only closed Amagansett food-service lease** in the record, 2095 Montauk Highway on the Napeague stretch, rented in June 2025 at **$180,000 base plus $40,000 NNN, about $40.21/SF base**, for a 4,476 SF off-core white box with an apartment, and Compass flags the closed price as "Unverified" [42]. The two Amagansett asks that bracket the market have sat **194 to 547 days without a price cut** [28][33][34]. The subject also has genuine physical limitations that the market would price: a kitchen "not much bigger than a bathroom," a wood building that "had problems," no on-site parking and an undersized lot [18][6]. After adjusting for these factors, the comparables support a market rent well below the raw $65–$85/SF Amagansett asks. Trophy retail (East Hampton Village at $145–$154/SF NNN, 136 Main Street Unit 1 at $192/SF, Sag Harbor small units at $161–$240/SF) is excluded from the conclusion except as an outer ceiling.

**Negotiating implication:** the evidence identifies no market-rent factor that would justify a **permanent** reduction below $5,000/month. The conservative, midpoint and aggressive market positions are **$5,500, $6,050 and $7,000/month** respectively. Any relief the landlord chooses to grant is better framed as temporary, documented tenant-economics relief (deferral with payback, tied to financial disclosure) than as a market reset. **Confidence is Low** on the precise dollar range and higher on the direction of the finding.

**The 2016–2017 lease assignment.** In December 2016 the Mary's Marvelous leasehold at 207 Main Street was publicly marketed by Douglas Elliman (Robert Kohr and Lori MacGarva). The listing text read: "This sale is 'key money' for the well established business, long term lease, and existing equipment; It does not include the business name or property" [9]. The asking key money was **$625,000** [9][10][12]. Broker Kohr said "There's only so many wet use stores out there" and that the deal "is essentially the same as buying a business, not a property" [12]. By June 2017 Behind the Hedges reported that "the lease, listed for $575K, has sold" [13][14], while 27east, on the same day, cited only the original $625,000 [15]. Organic Krush's co-founder said "the deal for purchasing the space was closed on Monday, June 12, for a price that she did not wish to discuss" [15]. The **closing price, contract rent, remaining term and lease type were never published.** Patch and Hamptons.com headlines describing "the property" as "sold" [16][17] mischaracterize what was a leasehold assignment; no fee transfer is recorded [3].

**What the key money implies (inference, with explicit assumptions).** A buyer pays key money to step into an existing lease only if the rent is below what a new lease would cost, or if the use entitlement, equipment and going-concern value are scarce. The executed-transactions research capitalized the asking key money at an assumed 10% discount rate over assumed remaining terms; the arithmetic is reproduced here as an ESTIMATE, not a finding:

| Key money (ask) | 10-year remaining term | 15-year | 20-year |
|---|---|---|---|
| $575,000 [13] | $93,579/yr ($7,798/mo) | $75,597/yr ($6,300/mo) | $67,539/yr ($5,628/mo) |

These figures are a **loose upper bound** on the 2017 gap between contract rent and market rent. Part of the payment bought used equipment, part bought the business's goodwill, and part bought the scarce wet-use status. Taken literally they would imply a 2017 market rent far above any current comparable, which itself shows that the key money was not a pure rent differential. The defensible conclusion is directional: **in 2016–2017 informed buyers valued this leasehold above its contract rent.** The illustration is not used in the market-rent calculation.

**Is today's $5,000 the same lease?** **Unconfirmed.** No source states whether the current $5,000/month derives from the lease assigned in 2017 (possibly with escalations), from a renewal, or from a new lease. If it is the assigned legacy lease, the 2017 key money is direct evidence that it was a below-market rent stream when assigned. If it is a newer lease, the key-money evidence speaks only to 2017 conditions. The landlord's own lease file resolves this, and it should be checked before the evidence is used in negotiation.

## Amagansett Market Overview

**Supply is extremely thin.** As of October 7, 2026, only **five verifiable current lease offerings** exist in Amagansett 11930, and three are floors of one building (11 Indian Wells Highway) [33][34][35][48]. The others are the 74A Montauk Highway restaurant [28] and 519–521 Montauk Highway, which quotes "rate upon request" and whose current status could not be verified [44][46]. The broker of record for most Amagansett offerings lists no other Amagansett lease in its 79-property sitemap [48]. The 500 SF end-cap at 136 Main Street (last ASK $96,000/year NNN, $192/SF) appears withdrawn or leased: its broker page returned 404 and it is absent from the broker's active list [39][40]. An automated LoopNet figure of "about $103 per square foot" for Amagansett retail is an aggregate of these few listings, not a comparable [49].

**Asking-rent tiers.** Current Amagansett ASKS run from **$35/SF NNN** (rear-access lower level) through **$50** (second floor) and **$65** (first-floor Main Street district flex) at 11 Indian Wells Highway [33][34][35] to **$84.81/SF NNN plus $700,000 key money** for a 2,830 SF, 126-seat turnkey restaurant with 31 parking spaces at 74A Montauk Highway [28]. The only CLOSED food-service lease, 2095 Montauk Highway on the Napeague stretch, works out to **$40.21/SF base plus $8.94/SF stated NNN** [42]. None of these is a close physical match for a 1,100 SF Main Street café; no current Main Street listing between 800 and 1,500 SF with a kitchen exists.

**Absorption signals cut both ways.** 74A has been offered for about 194 days and 11 Indian Wells Highway for about 316 days (first floor) to 547 days (ground level), all without a visible price cut [28][29][33][34]; a Crexi snippet suggests the first floor has been marketed even longer [37]. That points to asks at or above clearing levels for those configurations. On the other side, the Amagansett food-space record shows quick re-tenanting after exits: Hampton Chutney left Amagansett Square in 2021 after a rent offer "almost triple" its old rent, and Doubles opened in the space in July 2022 [76][24]; Main Street Tavern closed in 2022 and Rowdy Hall relocated to 177 Main Street in 2023 [24][83]; and the 136 Main Street frontage filled in 2026 with Sett Coffee, Sea Dragon Books (on a two-year lease), Jamagansett and LaParlour [86].

**Demand indicators are positive.** The East Hampton Star reported in July 2026 that Amagansett's west end, "once dead as a desert," had "come to life this summer" [86]. Town Board members in March 2026 cited "increasing foot traffic" and said "Amagansett is happening right now" [104][105]. New food and beverage entrants include Amagansett Coffee Co. (April 2026) [115] and the Meeting House reopening in Amagansett Square [119]. Region-wide, a Saunders broker said in 2026 that "Since then [COVID], the villages, particularly East Hampton and Sag Harbor, haven't had many" vacancies [100]; The Real Deal reported in 2025 that "landlords are able to choose what tenants they want because there's limited supply" [101]; and Hamptons median sale prices rose 18% in Q1 2026, with a record $39 million retail sale in May 2026 [102]. Suffolk County sales-tax receipts rose **9.2%** in the first half of 2026, although the State Comptroller attributes part of that to higher prices and warns it "may not last" [103].

**Headwinds are real but mostly operator- and industry-level.** Coche Comedor closed at 74A on September 27, 2025, with its operator saying "running a seasonal business, especially a restaurant in the Hamptons, has grown increasingly difficult" [78]. Nationally, more than 60% of restaurant operators reported traffic declines in 2025 [108]. The Long Island minimum wage rose to $17.00 in 2026 [106][107], but it will **not** rise in 2027 because the statutory off-ramp was triggered [106]. Seasonality is structural: in February 2023 Organic Krush was "Closed for the winter," and an Amagansett operator described winter closure as "a really big no-brainer" given fixed costs [24].

**Regulatory supply constraint.** The CB district and historic district restrict new commercial space; in 2022 the Planning Board found a 12-storefront proposal at 136 Main Street out of scale [123], and the Town's hamlet study recommends retaining the present CB configuration [8][124]. Restricted supply of permitted food-service premises supports market rent for existing wet-use space such as the subject [10].

**Montauk notes.** Montauk shows the deepest cyclical discounting in the record: 20–30% lease-ask cuts in 2009 [88], a sale-price cut at 716 Montauk Highway from $8.9 million (2016) to $6.7 million (March 2017) [97], and a Martell's / Montauk Fuels sale-ask series from $10.5 million (2016) to $5.475 million (2021) [127]. In 2019, Montauk restaurant prices were described as "not as steep" as elsewhere [79].

**What the history shows.** First, Hamptons retail rents are cyclical: East Hampton Village asks fell from about $250 to $110–$140 between 2008 and 2011, then recovered [88][89]. Second, the post-2020 period is a step-up: an Amagansett renewal offer at about 3x (2021) [76], East End rents +9.8% in a year (2022) [94], and an Amagansett restaurant building up about 58% in under three years [81][80]. Third, **Amagansett turnover has been driven by landlord rent resets and sales, and vacated spaces have re-let within about a season** (Outdoors in 2012, Gone Local in 2013, Hampton Chutney in 2021, 177 Main in 2020–2023) [125][73][76][24]. The exception is 519–521 Montauk Highway, which is outside the walkable core [45]. Fourth, **seasonality is priced explicitly**: a pop-up season costs about two-thirds to three-quarters of a year's rent [91], and the 2026 74A seasonal ask is 72.9% of its annual ask [28]. A lease negotiated in 2002 or assigned in 2017 would have been set before the post-2020 step-up.

## Comparable Adjustments

**Normalization conventions (applied before the grid).**

1. **Rent basis.** All comparables are compared on **annual NNN base rent per SF**. Where a comparable is gross or a pop-up figure, an NNN load is subtracted.
2. **NNN load assumption: $7.00/SF (range $5.00–$8.75).** Sourcing: real estate taxes from broker-published tax bills run **$1.38–$3.22/SF** on small village commercial buildings [68][69][70][71]; the subject's own taxes are about $2.96/SF [3]; taxes alone at 74A are $4.92/SF [31]; the one stated all-in Amagansett NNN figure is **$8.94/SF** at 2095 Montauk Highway [42]; taxes at 136 Main / 11 Indian Wells run about $1.31–$2.02/SF [36]; and a generic national benchmark puts retail NNN at $4–$8/SF [72]. Insurance and maintenance components (about $1.00–$2.50 and $1.00–$3.00/SF respectively) are **unsourced assumptions** and should be replaced with the subject's actual figures.
3. **Seasonal to annual.** A seasonal rent is converted to an annual-equivalent by **dividing by 0.67–0.75** (midpoint 0.71), following the broker rule that a 4–6 month season costs "two-thirds to three-fourths of the annual asking rent" [91], which 74A's 2026 asks confirm (72.9%) [28]. Scaling by months occupied is **not** used, because it overstates annual rent by 33–125%. The premium attaches to summer-only occupancy specifically: a 2015 Bridgehampton offering priced month-to-month at the same monthly rate as its annual lease ($10,000 versus $120,000 a year) [126].
4. **Transaction status (ask to achieved).** Asks on market more than about 180 days without a cut: **−10%** (74A, 11 Indian Wells, 9 Railroad Ave). Other current asks and stale historical asks: **−5%**. Closed leases: **0%**. The spread is moderate because the 2025–2026 market is tight [100][101], while 2009 spreads reached 20–25% [88] and long marketing periods here signal resistance [28][33].
5. **Market timing.** A 2021 figure is moved **+10%** to 2026, a conservative reading of East End rents rising 9.8% in a single year to November 2022 [94] and Long Island retail asks rising about 9% year over year in 2026 [113]. Figures from 2025–2026 are not time-adjusted.
6. **Size.** Smaller spaces command higher rent per SF: spaces under 1,000 SF cost "proportionately more" [87], and in 2026 a 400 SF Sag Harbor unit asks $240/SF against $125/SF for a 1,200 SF unit nearby [63]. Those trophy spreads imply an elasticity of about 0.3–0.6. This report uses **0.15**, so that size adjustment = (comparable SF / 1,100)^0.15 − 1. Restaurant rent is driven more by seats and back-of-house than by frontage ("restaurants are valued on the basis of the number of tables" [98]), so a conservative elasticity is appropriate.
7. **Key money.** Key money is consideration paid for occupancy on top of base rent, and it is routine for turnkey Hamptons restaurant leases: $225,000 to take over the Springs Tavern lease (2021) [117], $500,000 for the Wölffer Kitchen Sag Harbor lease (2020) [116], $250,000 at 17 S Edison [50] and $700,000 at 74A [28]. It is converted to a per-SF annual amount on a straight-line basis over an assumed 10-year term, and **only 50% is treated as rent-equivalent**. The other 50% is assumed to pay for equipment and goodwill, since the 74A key money "would provide ownership of all of the equipment" [28]. The 50% share is an assumption.

**Why each percentage was chosen.**

**Location, visibility and frontage.** The subject is an at-grade corner storefront in the Main Street core, next to the Town lot and the library [2][4][18]. 74A also fronts NY-27 at the west end of the hamlet with highway visibility, so it gets 0 for location, with its pedestrian disadvantage captured under foot traffic. 11 Indian Wells sits at the Main Street corner but is entered by "a welcoming entry staircase from the sidewalk" [33], so the subject's at-grade storefront earns +5%. 2095 Montauk Highway is a roadside site about six miles east of the hamlet on the Napeague stretch [42]; +15% reflects the core-versus-off-core spread, which historically ran to 35–65% between village tiers [89] and is applied here conservatively. Downtown Montauk is treated as equivalent to Amagansett's core (0). East Hampton Village and Southampton Village have larger year-round trade areas than Amagansett, so 9 Railroad Ave and 7 Hill St each take −5%.

**Foot traffic.** The subject's pedestrian position (+5% versus 74A and versus 2095's drive-to stretch) and Railroad Avenue's secondary-street status (+5%) are modest. Downtown Montauk, near the beach among "some of the region's highest-grossing restaurant destinations" [50], has stronger summer volume (−5%).

**Size.** These figures come from the 0.15 elasticity formula: 2,830 SF gives +15%, 2,480 SF +13%, 4,476 SF +23%, 2,729 SF +15%, 3,000 SF +16%, 925 SF −3%, and 1,000 SF ground floor −1%.

**Parking.** The subject has no on-site parking, but adjoins a Town lot planned at about 185 time-limited spaces [25]. Dedicated on-site parking is superior: 74A has 31 spaces [28], 2095 has "an ample parking lot" [42] and 9 Railroad has "ample parking" [57], each −10%. 11 Indian Wells shares a 33-space lot among many tenants [36], so −5%. 17 S Edison has only a side alley for delivery and employee parking [50], so the subject's lot adjacency is superior (+5%).

**Patio.** 74A has an "expansive open patio" with bar and 30 outdoor seats [28], and 2095 has 450 SF of approved outdoor seating plus other outdoor space [42], each −5%. Where no outdoor space is stated (249 Main, 9 Railroad, 7 Hill St), the subject's patio earns +5%.

**Kitchen and restaurant readiness.** The subject's kitchen is very small [18], so comparables with large kitchens take −10%: 74A has an "oversized kitchen," "multiple walk-ins" and a basement legal prep area [28], and 17 S Edison has a "large kitchen" and a 112 SF walk-in freezer [50]. 2095 has double hoods but is delivered as a "clean white box" without furniture, fixtures or equipment [42]; these offset (0). Dry spaces with no wet-use entitlement (11 Indian Wells, 9 Railroad, 249 Main) take +10% for the subject's scarce wet use [10][12]; 7 Hill St permits only "modified wet uses" [64], so +5%. The wet-use premium is held to 10% because, within a given village, restaurant boxes often ask less per SF than small dry shops. Montauk's restaurant asks $57.16 [50] against $80.69 for dry retail [96], and Southampton's grocery space asks $77.42 [66] against small shops above $100 [65]. The difference largely reflects size and back-of-house area, and key money captures the turnkey value.

**Condition and historic status.** Comparables marketed as renovated or fully built-out take −5% against a subject whose operator recalls constant repairs and whose current condition is Unknown [18]: 74A ("Renovated in 2020... central A/C") [28], 11 Indian Wells ("full build-out," elevator) [33], 9 Railroad (renovated as a gallery) [57] and 7 Hill St (renovated complex) [65]. No adjustment is made for historic-district ARB constraints alone, since 11 Indian Wells and 249 Main share them [4].

**Signage and zoning.** All seven permit the comparable's current use; the subject's ARB sign limits are offset by its core position (0).

**Storage and ancillary space.** 74A includes a finished lower level, legal prep area and office (−5%) [28]. 2095 includes a three-bedroom apartment and 1.1 acres (−10%) [42].

**Seasonality.** This applies to the hamlet's market, not to the tenant's chosen hours. The Napeague stretch depends on resort traffic (+5% to the subject). Year-round village populations in East Hampton and Southampton (−5%) are an appraiser judgment consistent with the 2011 observation that the "prime shopping season is just three months" in summer-driven locations [89].

**Key money.** 74A: $700,000 ÷ 10 years ÷ 2,830 SF = $24.73/SF; 50% = $12.37/SF, about 16% of the normalized $76.33, rounded to **+15%** [28]. 17 S Edison: $250,000 ÷ 10 ÷ 2,729 = $9.16/SF; 50% = $4.58/SF, about 8% of $54.30, so **+8%** [50].

## Tenant Rent vs. Market

**Finding: BELOW MARKET.**

**Based on the weighted comparable analysis, estimated market rent is $60–$72/SF/year (NNN), implying $5,500–$6,600/month; the existing $5,000/month is approximately 17% below the estimated market midpoint of $6,050/month** ($54.55 versus $66.00/SF). It is about 9% below the low end of the range and about 24% below the high end. If the lease is gross, the shortfall is about 25% on a gross-equivalent basis.

The finding is honest about the evidence base, which is thinner and lower than a reading of Amagansett asking rents alone would suggest. **The most relevant Amagansett restaurant ask is $84.81/SF NNN at 74A Montauk Highway, and it comes with $700,000 of key money** [28]. Main Street district dry space asks **$65/SF NNN** [33]. But **the only closed Amagansett food-service lease is about $40/SF base in Napeague** for a much larger, off-core, white-box space with an apartment [42]. Asking rents of $65–$85/SF in this market have not cleared quickly [28][33], and the subject's tiny kitchen, condition history and lack of on-site parking [18] are genuine market discounts that are already built into the grid. After those adjustments, the defensible range is **$60–$72**, not the $65–$85 that raw asks imply. The direction of the finding is reinforced by independent qualitative evidence: the 2017 key money for this leasehold [15], the "almost triple" Amagansett Square renewal [76], and a tight post-2020 market [100][101].

**How far below market the current rent is.** At 1,100 SF and NNN, the current rent is **$500/month ($6,000/year) below the conservative position**, **$1,050/month ($12,600/year) below the midpoint** and **$2,000/month ($24,000/year) below the aggressive position**. **Position on the tenant's request for a reduction.** The evidence identifies no adverse change in market-rent factors that would support a **permanent** reduction below $5,000/month, which is already below the reconciled market range. The aggressive position rests on an ask with key money and on a 50% key-money allocation assumption; it is a credible opening only if the landlord is prepared to defend those assumptions. The midpoint is the recommended reset target at renewal or at any re-opening of terms. The conservative figure is the floor that the evidence can defend against a well-advised tenant's broker.

**Structural alternatives.**

- **Temporary concession, not a permanent reset.** If the tenant documents hardship, a deferral of part of the rent for a defined period (for example, one off-season), repaid over the following season, preserves the rent level. Relief should be conditioned on delivery of store-level sales and profit-and-loss statements. The 2009 local precedent of "teaser rates, giving a discount for the first two or three years with the stipulation the rent will go up later" shows the market has used front-loaded concessions with built-in step-ups [88].
- **Permanent reset to market at renewal.** If the lease is near expiry or has a renewal option, the market range supports resetting to $5,500–$6,600/month, with "annual increases" as the stated Hamptons norm [98]. No Hamptons source specifies an escalation percentage.
- **Percentage rent.** Percentage rent is not documented as common for small Hamptons storefronts; key money is the usual mechanism for capturing location value. A public precedent exists: the towns' 2011 Poxabogue restaurant RFP set a fixed rent plus 10% of gross if greater [131]. A structure of $5,000 base plus a percentage of gross sales above a breakpoint would let the landlord share in a strong season without raising fixed rent. It requires audited sales reporting.
- **Term extension in exchange for stability.** "Landlords want five years with an option to renew and annual increases" [98]. Granting a longer term at the current rent in exchange for scheduled step-ups to market, a personal or corporate guaranty, and year-round or minimum operating covenants trades rent level for occupancy security. Given the chain's contraction, this may be worth more to the landlord than a higher face rent.
- **NNN conversion.** If the current lease is gross, converting to NNN would shift the subject's taxes (about $3,261/year, about $272/month) [3] plus insurance and maintenance to the tenant. That adds about $5–$8.75/SF of value (ESTIMATE) without changing headline base rent. **NNN is the overwhelming quoting convention in 2026 Hamptons storefront and restaurant listings**: about 16 of the readable listings say "+ NNN" and none says "gross" [28][33][50][51][56][57][58][62][63][64][66].

## Potential Tenant Arguments and Responses

**Governing definition.** Market rent is "The most probable rent that a property should bring in a competitive and open market under all conditions requisite to a fair lease transaction, the lessee and lessor each acting prudently and knowledgeably, and assuming the rent is not affected by undue stimulus," with lessee and lessor "typically motivated" and the rent reflecting terms "typically found in that market, such as permitted uses, use restrictions, expense obligations, duration, concessions" [109]. The same Appraisal Institute article distinguishes "use rent" tied to a specific use and says it "is never synonymous with market rent" [109]. The federal banking definition of market value at **12 CFR § 323.2(h)** uses the same framework: "the most probable price which a property should bring in a competitive and open market under all conditions requisite to a fair sale, the buyer and seller each acting prudently and knowledgeably, and assuming the price is not affected by undue stimulus," with buyer and seller "typically motivated" [110]. Under both definitions, a **MARKET-RENT factor** affects what any typical tenant would pay for these premises. A **TENANT-ECONOMICS factor** is specific to this operator's results and does not change market rent.

## Conclusion

The question is not whether 207 Main Street could command trophy-village rent; it cannot, and nothing in this assessment relies on that. The question is whether a typically motivated, well-advised food-service operator would pay more than $54.55/SF for this small, entitled, historic Main Street box next to the hamlet's main parking lot. The weight of the evidence says yes, but by roughly $5–$18/SF rather than by $30 or more. The landlord's strongest argument is not an asking rent. It is the documented market behavior around this exact space: a leasehold that commanded six-figure key money and multiple inquiries, in a hamlet where vacated food spaces re-let within a season and where a neighboring landlord priced a legacy restaurant renewal at about three times its old rent.

The weakest link in the landlord's case is that the record contains only one closed Amagansett food-service rent, and it is low. A tenant's broker will build its case on 2095 Montauk Highway and on the subject's physical limits. The reconciled range answers that by pricing those limits explicitly rather than ignoring them. Before any negotiation, three facts held by the landlord or obtainable at modest cost would change the confidence level more than further market research: the lease's type and expense allocation, a measured floor area, and whether the current lease is the one assigned in 2017.
