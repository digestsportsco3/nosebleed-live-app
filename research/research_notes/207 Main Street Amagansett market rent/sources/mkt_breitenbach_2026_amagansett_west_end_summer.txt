Amagansett's Center of Gravity Moved West This Summer. Here's the Map.

For years the walkable core of Amagansett was a short arc that began at Amagansett Square and ended at Amber Waves Farm. The block west of Hedges Lane, anchored by the Mobil station and a 1920s building that spent years between tenants, was the stretch you looked at through a windshield on the way somewhere else. That is no longer the case. As of summer 2026, the hamlet's commercial center runs the full length of Main Street, the south-side sidewalk is being physically rebuilt in brick, and a coffee shop, a bookstore, a restaurant, and a salon now sit within thirty feet of one another at the western entrance. If you live here, the shape of your Saturday morning has quietly changed.
The Block That Changed
The 1920s building at 136 Main Street is the pivot. Four years ago its owners floated a proposal that would have leveled it along with the red barn behind it. What arrived instead is a cluster of small operators who treat the block as a neighborhood rather than a stopover.
On a Saturday morning the crowd at SETT skews under forty, with strollers and dogs and, per the East Hampton Star, conversations about Little League and the price of peanut butter at the I.G.A. That is not a destination crowd. That is a Tuesday-through-Sunday crowd.
Two facts explain why the shift feels permanent rather than seasonal. First, the operators are local. John and Kelly Piccinnini, who own the Clam Bar and previously ran The Corner in Sag Harbor, opened SETT Coffee here. Sea Dragon Books next door is run by Tricia Hoefling and Charles Billings with their mother, all Sagaponack residents who decided the hamlet needed a bookstore. Second, the sidewalk itself is being upgraded, which tells you the town expects foot traffic to justify the spend.
What the New Map Looks Like
Read east to west along the south side of Main Street, the corridor now stacks like this:
Amber Waves Farm at the east end, still the anchor for produce, prepared food, and the farm-stand rhythm that defines the hamlet.
Amagansett Square, home to Doubles and the Thursday afternoon farmers market.
The old center block between Hedges Lane and Windmill Lane, where Il Buco al Mare and the longstanding retail sit.
The west-end cluster at and around 136 Main Street: SETT Coffee, Sea Dragon Books, a restaurant, and a salon within a thirty-foot radius.
The point is not that any single opening is transformative. The point is that you can now walk the whole thing without a break in the sequence, which was not true two winters ago.
The Openings Worth Knowing This Season
Four names matter for how you actually spend your week.
SETT Coffee
The Piccinninis' coffee, matcha, and pastry counter has become the west end's living room. Watch for their partnership with The Mom Club, which is running a series of guided conversations with childhood experts through the season. If you have kids under school age and you are tired of the drive to Springs for a stroller-friendly cafe, this is your answer.
Doubles, 12 Amagansett Square
Brothers Daniel and Evan Bennett, who also run Mimi and Babs in Greenwich Village, took the space Hampton Chutney Co. vacated after two decades. Doubles is Caribbean-influenced, and it is worth registering the shift in behavior: a year ago it was open four days a week and treated as a novelty. In 2026 it is on more residents' regular rotation, which is the local metric that matters.
LUNCH Lobster Roll, 1980 Montauk Highway
The Amagansett location opened for the 2026 season on Friday, April 24, operating Thursday through Monday from 11:45 a.m. through spring and expanding to seven days a week in mid-June. The interesting fact about LUNCH is that nothing about it has changed, which in a food scene that reinvents itself annually is its own form of information. The cold-water lobster meat mixed with a house mayo dressing and celery is the same roll it was a decade ago. Bring guests here when you want to reset expectations about what the East End used to feel like.
Nourish at The Roundtree
This one is technically in Bridgehampton, at 203 Bridgehampton-Sag Harbor Turnpike in the former Arthur & Sons space, but it is a Roundtree Amagansett project from owner Sylvia Wong. Executive chef Dewa Wijaya, Bali-born and French-trained, opened on May 18 with a menu that pairs East End produce with Balinese influence: crispy catch-of-the-day sushi bites, salt-baked branzino, grilled Acabonac Farms ribeye. Takuma Watanabe of Martiny's and Odo Hospitality runs the bar program, which includes house-made kombucha and alcohol-free wines alongside seasonal cocktails. Worth the fifteen minutes west when you want the Amagansett hospitality DNA without a hamlet-center reservation battle.

The Church's summer exhibition, curated around American landscape painting and its afterlives, is the one to walk through slowly. If you have not been in since spring, the light in the sanctuary reads differently in August, and the show rewards a second visit.
The Weekly Rhythm
The recurring events are the ones that shape a resident's week more than the marquee dates. Keep these on your calendar:
Amagansett Square Farmers Market, Thursdays 3:30 to 6 p.m. through September, with live busking.
Morning Yoga in the Square, Saturdays 9 to 10 a.m. through July and August, outdoor all-levels flow.
Thursday Tai Chi at LongHouse Reserve with Katherine Henderson, alternating Thursdays from May to September, 10 to 11 a.m.
Two of the three are free. All three are within a five-minute drive of one another. If you live here full-time, stacking a Thursday afternoon market run with a Thursday Tai Chi session on the alternating week is the kind of small rearrangement the west-end shift makes easier.
Why the Shift Matters if You Actually Live Here
Three practical consequences.
The parking pressure that used to concentrate at Amagansett Square now distributes across a longer corridor. That is a Tuesday-morning improvement, not a Saturday one, but it counts.
The mix of businesses on the west end skews toward daily needs rather than seasonal indulgence. A coffee shop, a bookstore, and a salon operate on a different rhythm than a rosé bar. That is what "walkable center" means in a hamlet whose year-round population is a fraction of its August one.
And the physical infrastructure investment, the brick sidewalk rebuild on the south side, signals that the town is treating this as durable rather than provisional. When municipal capital follows private capital, the arrangement tends to hold.
None of this changes what makes Amagansett what it is. Modesty is still the local dialect. As one recent local guide put it about the dining scene, the food is the award, and the line is the only reservation. But the geography of how you use the hamlet, the specific corners you visit on a Wednesday errand run, has shifted. Update your route accordingly.
